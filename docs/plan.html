1. The blunt diagnosis (read this first)

You said you don't know your weakness. Here's the uncomfortable statistical truth about the 1600–1900 Lichess band, before I even see your games: you are almost certainly not losing because of openings, and definitely not because of a lack of "advanced strategy." You are losing because pieces get left hanging, one-move tactics get missed (yours and your opponent's), and won positions get thrown away. This is true of nearly everyone at your level, including people who feel like their problem is "I don't know what to do in the middlegame."

The players who escape 1750 are not the ones who memorize a sharper Najdorf line. They are the ones who stop giving away material for free and who see the opponent's threats before moving. That is a trainable habit, not talent. So the plan below is weighted accordingly: the bulk of your hours go to tactics, calculation discipline, and reviewing your own losses — not to opening theory, which is the most over-invested and lowest-ROI activity at your level.

3. The core method (the part most people skip)

Three habits do more than any book. If you did only these and nothing else in the plan, you would still gain rating:

A. The pre-move blunder check — every move, no exceptions

Before you play any move, run a 3-second scan. Train it until it's automatic:

C.C.T. — Checks, Captures, Threats
  • After my move, what are ALL my opponent's checks, captures, and threats? Especially: can they take something of mine for free or win it with a tactic?
  • Is the piece I'm moving, or the square I'm leaving, creating a hanging piece or a loose defender?
  • Only then: is this the move I want?

In 5+0 this has to become a fast reflex, not a deliberate pause — so you build it first in untimed puzzles and unrated slow games, then compress it. Most blunders at 1750 are not "I couldn't calculate it" — they're "I never looked." Killing the 0.65 blunders/game this catches is the single highest-value habit on this page.

B. Analyze every loss yourself before turning on the engine

After a loss, open the game and — engine OFF — find where you think it went wrong and what you'd play instead. Write one sentence: "I lost because ___." Then turn the engine on (Lichess "Request a computer analysis" / "Learn from your mistakes") and compare. The gap between your explanation and the engine's is your actual curriculum. Skipping the engine-off step is why most people review games and learn nothing.

C. Review beats volume — five blitz games you analyze beat fifty you don't

You're going to keep playing blitz — good, that's your ladder. But blitz only rewards the habits you already have; it doesn't build new ones, because you never have time to think. So the building happens off the clock: in puzzles, and in reviewing the blitz games you played. One optional accelerant — a couple of slow, unrated training games a week where you run the full blunder-check deliberately — hard-wires the reflex faster than blitz alone ever will. The climb is in blitz; the learning is in review and puzzles.

5. Skill modules & resources

You don't need all of these — pick one resource per category and finish it before adding another. Tags mark how confident I am each is well-suited to a 1750 blitz improver.

Tactics & board vision core

  • Lichess Puzzles (free, built in) — set them to your level; do 15–25/day. Turn on Puzzle Streak once a week to train not-blundering-under-consequence.
  • The Woodpecker Method (Smith & Tikkanen) — a structured system of solving the same tactic set repeatedly to build recognition speed. Ideal for exactly your problem. high fit
  • 1001 Chess Exercises for Club Players (Frank Erwich) or Chess: 5334 Problems (László Polgár) for volume. good
  • Chess.com Puzzle Rush / Puzzle Storm — timed pattern reps; fun and effective in small doses.

Calculation & candidate moves core

  • Practice on Lichess puzzles rated slightly above you where you calculate fully before moving — no guess-and-click. Name your candidate moves out loud, pick, then verify.
  • Improve Your Chess Calculation (R.B. Ramesh) — a modern, well-regarded calculation trainer. stretch but valuable

Endgames a real second front — ~26% of your blunders (engine-corrected)

I under-called this earlier. The Stockfish pass shows about a quarter of your blunders happen in the endgame — mostly under time pressure in long games. You don't need deep study, but you do need enough automatic technique that endgames don't eat clock and produce scrambles.

  • Lock down the essentials cold, so they cost you zero thinking time: King & pawn opposition, the square rule, Lucena & Philidor rook endings, basic K+Q vs K and K+R vs K mates, and which rook endings are drawn a pawn down.
  • Work the "1800–1999" section of Silman's Complete Endgame Course — one section a week for a few weeks, then maintain. The goal is speed and automaticity, not depth.
  • Practical blitz habit: when you reach a won or equal endgame, spend a couple of extra seconds — that's where your 26% is bleeding out.

Strategy, structures & classic games supporting

  • Logical Chess: Move by Move (Chernev) — every move of every game explained. Perfect bridge from "I don't know what to do" to having plans. high fit
  • Simple Chess (Michael Stean) — short, clear intro to positional ideas (weak squares, outposts, open files). good
  • The Amateur's Mind (Silman) — diagnoses exactly the thinking errors club players make. good

Openings — deliberately minimal low priority

Do not rabbit-hole here. At 1750 you need a simple, complete, principle-based repertoire you understand — not deep theory. Goal: reach a playable middlegame you know the plans for, every game.
  • Pick one response to 1.e4, one to 1.d4, and one White opening. Solid, plan-based choices (e.g. as White the London or an Italian/Ruy setup; as Black the Caro-Kann or an ...e5 system, and a King's Indian or Queen's Gambit Declined vs 1.d4). Any of these is fine — pick and stick.
  • Use free Chessable "Short & Sweet" courses and the Lichess Opening Explorer to learn the first 6–10 moves and, more importantly, the typical plan and pawn breaks.
  • Build a personal Lichess Study with your lines and the ideas in your own words. Update it when a game leaves you unsure what to do.

Coaches / structured programs & video optional but strong

  • Daniel Naroditsky's "Rating Climb" / speedrun videos (YouTube, free) — arguably the best free improver content; he narrates the exact thinking process you're trying to build. high fit
  • ChessDojo — a structured training program with rating-band "cohorts" and prescribed tasks. If you want the plan below run for you with accountability, this is the closest paid option. worth considering
  • Saint Louis Chess Club lectures and Hanging Pawns (openings/strategy) for depth when you want it.
  • A few sessions with a real coach (even one/month) to review your games is the fastest accelerant if budget allows.

Analytics tools

  • Lichess Insights (built in) — filter your losses by phase, opening, time-trouble. Tells you objectively where points leak.
  • Aimchess — automated report on your recurring weaknesses across recent games. A useful second opinion to what I'll produce from your games.

8. The five rules, if you forget everything else

  1. Look at your opponent's checks, captures, and threats before every move. Your data says this one habit is worth more than everything else combined. Drill it slow, run it fast.
  2. Do tactics daily. Small and consistent beats binges — and speed here is what makes the check survive 5+0.
  3. Review every loss yourself before the engine tells you.
  4. Climb in blitz, learn off the clock. Your rating goes up in blitz; the reflex gets built in puzzles, review, and the odd slow training game.
  5. Stop studying openings past move 8. Spend that time not hanging pieces instead.
